AI-native, explained

What an AI-native operator is.

Not a job title. A way of running the store, and you can tell one from the outside in about a minute.

If your week starts by reading what the agents found, and your changes ship with a before and after attached, you're already one. The rest of this page is how to get there on purpose.
AI-native operatornoun

Someone who runs an ecommerce site through agents that read the store's own data, keeps the judgment for themselves, and can show the before and after of every change.

The shift

From somewhere you go to something your tools can ask.

For years the data lived in a dashboard. Someone had to log in, know which tab to trust, know which big number wasn't a bug count. That knowledge lived in two heads per company. Here is what changes.

Before
  • A dashboard you go to. Two people know how to read it.
  • Problems arrive as customer emails, three days late.
  • The developer backlog is the bottleneck. Every idea waits a sprint.
  • Changes ship without evidence. Did it work? Nobody can say.
  • Every tool has its own memory of your store, and none of them agree.
After
  • You ask the store a question in plain English and the answer comes back ranked by money.
  • Problems arrive in the morning run, ranked, with the fix already drafted.
  • Finding to shipping in one sitting. The bottleneck is knowing what to do, which is your job.
  • Every change gets a release marker and a before and after.
  • One vault, written by you, that every tool reads first.
Five principles

How an AI-native operator works.

Your data, in plain English

Questions go to the store. Answers come back from your own sessions, errors and orders, ranked by what they cost you.

Finding to shipping

Analysis that doesn't end in a change is a report. A finding should become a pull request, a theme change or a test in the same sitting.

Agents do the week

The morning run, the weekly scan, the release check. You read the summary and make the calls. You don't do the looking.

One vault, every tool

The context lives in one portable file you own, not in a vendor's memory. Every tool reads it first. You can leave with it.

Evidence or it didn't happen

Every change ships with a marker and a before and after. Your agency gets requirements and results, not hunches.

What it isn't

It isn't a chatbot bolted onto your dashboard. It isn't replacing your team; it's giving the two people who knew everything a way to write it down and let agents use it. It isn't buying more tools, and most operators end up with fewer. And it isn't a dev project: the first two weeks of the path need no developer at all.

Terms we use

The vocabulary.

Eight words that come up in every piece. Operators' words, not vendors'.

vault
Your plain-text knowledge base about the store. What you sell, what normal looks like, what's been tried, who decides. Every AI tool reads it first.
morning run
A scheduled agent that reports what broke overnight, ranked by lost orders, with the fixes most worth your time already drafted.
store pulse
A daily read on conversion, speed and errors against last week, by device. Two minutes to read.
weekly scan
A scheduled pass over errors and speed that opens issues for the top few. Issue first, then fix.
release marker
A timestamp posted to monitoring every time code ships, so you can see what a release changed.
skill
A packaged, repeatable job for an agent: a file you install once and run by name. "Run the morning run."
agent
An AI that can read your store's data and act on it (open an issue, draft a PR, start a test), on a schedule or when asked.
connector
What lets an agent read a system: your monitoring, your platform, your repo. The plumbing. You should never have to think about it after setup.

The Five A's

Five things an AI-native operator does every week.

In order. Each one is a habit, and each one depends on the one before it. The spine for everything in the library.

Most stores live at Ask. The jump that matters is Activate. The one that changes the job is Automate. Anticipate is where you stop hearing about problems from customers.
Ask

Put a question to your store in plain English and get the answer from your own data, not a dashboard.

e.g. what's broken on checkout right now, ranked by lost orders?
Analyze

Go from the answer to the cause. The session, the error, the page, the campaign that sent the traffic.

e.g. show me sessions where the cart drawer didn't open.
Activate

Ship it. A pull request, a theme change, an A/B test. Finding to live in the same sitting.

e.g. open a PR that stops lazy-loading the first row of products.
Automate

Schedule it. The morning run, the weekly scan, the release check. Agents do the week, you read the summary.

e.g. every Monday at 1am, top two errors, top two speed issues, open the issues.
Anticipate

Have the agents watch for what's coming: the drop, the sale, the traffic spike. Hear about it before it costs orders.

e.g. before Friday's launch, test the product page on mobile.

Four weeks to AI-native.

One hour a day. No developer until week three. Sixteen things to tick off, and this page remembers your ticks on this device.

Do the weeks in order. Week one is the boring one and it's the one that makes the rest work. If you skip the vault, every tool you use starts from zero every time.

The one skill

Build your vault first.

Your vault is a plain-text knowledge base about your store: what you sell, what normal looks like, what's been tried, who owns what, which tools you run. Every AI tool you touch reads it first.

It's portable and it's yours. It's the difference between an assistant that guesses and one that knows your store. And it's the one thing on this site that is available today, because it's a file, not a product.

Why a file, and why yours

Every tool you use is building its own memory of your store, and none of them agree. The vault moves that context out of vendors' memories and into one file you control. It lives in your repo or your project folder. It's versioned. You can leave with it.

Keep it under two pages. Plain text, no secrets, one owner, updated weekly. The morning run can append to it. The agency can read it. New hires read it on day one and know more than most people learn in a quarter.

How to use it

Put store.md in the root of your theme repo, or in the project folder of whatever AI tool you work in. Start every session with "read store.md before answering anything". When an agent gets something wrong, fix the vault, not the prompt.

# store.md · read this before answering anything

what normal isCR 2.1–2.4% weekday, 2.8% on promo days, AOV $142
platformShopify, theme in GitHub, agency merges, we approve
don't touchcheckout extensions, the gift app, anything in /apps

toolsads, email, analytics, monitoring. One of each, no overlap

The format

Nine sections. Each one stops an agent doing something dumb.

what we sell

Category, SKU count, price range, markets, the split between mobile and desktop. One paragraph.

what normal looks like

Conversion, AOV, speed and error rates for a normal week, by device. So "is this bad?" has an answer.

platform and plumbing

Platform, where the theme lives, who merges, how a change gets to production.

don't touch

The checkout extensions, the apps, the templates that break if an agent edits them. Be specific.

tried already

Every test and fix, with the result. Agents stop proposing the thing you killed in March.

who decides

Names, not roles. Who approves a PR, who owns ads, who signs anything over a number.

tools

What you run and what each one is for. Every category once. Overlap is where money and confusion go.

this month

The one thing that matters right now, and the things that are off the table because of it.

our words

What you call things. Collections, drops, the names of your customer segments. Agents should use your words.

Copy it. Fill it in. Point your tools at it.

Cycling retailer · UKActivate · Automate

A hundred release markers a fortnight

For years the monitoring was a dashboard that two people knew how to read. Then the developers started living in Claude Code and GitHub, and the monitoring moved in with them. Every merge posts a release marker automatically. After a big change the agent keeps watching and comes back if something new appears. When a customer rings to say the site wouldn't let them pay, they read out a short code and the team is watching their session.

The best example never threw an error: a wrong currency on a price, page looked perfect. A label search found every customer it hit. That's the shift from "did it error" to "was it right".

About

How this is run.

A community program with a practitioner council. Small on purpose. Here's the model, the rules, and the money.

We looked hard at the alliances that came before this one. The ones that worked educated a market. The ones that went wrong grew a payroll. We're building the first kind.

The model

Two co-founders and seven contributors. One piece a week in the library, one session a month, a podcast that was already running. Contributors are named on what they make and have a say in what gets published. There's no legal entity, no board, no membership tiers and no certification. If that ever needs to change, it'll be because the people here asked for it. The model isn't ours; six other professions got their name the same way.

The money

Noibu is the founding sponsor. It pays for the site, the production and the co-founders' time so that everything here can be free. It gets its name in the footer and nothing on the shelf. No paid placements, no sponsored pieces, no logos on stage. Where a Noibu tool appears in a piece it's one option among several, and the piece has to work with at least one other.

The rules

  • Operators first. If it isn't useful to someone running a store this week, it doesn't go up.
  • Everything leaves with you. A file, a prompt, a video. Nothing lives behind a login.
  • Names on work. Contributors speak for themselves and are never asked to recommend anything.
  • One option among several. Any tool named has to be replaceable in the piece.
  • If it stops being useful, it stops. We review at ninety days and say so either way.

Contact

Reply to any weekly drop and a co-founder reads it. For anything else, the contributor form works for humans too.

Why this works

Others did it first. Here's what we're taking.

Every profession that got a name got it from a community that named it. We studied the ones that worked, in other industries, and borrowed the parts that fit a group of operators with no budget for an org chart.

Figures are as the sources state them; the sources are linked on each card. None of these were quick. The fastest took three years to feel real.
Customer success · Gainsight · 2013Name the person

From 300 people to a profession

When Gainsight started there were "no tools, no job titles, no conferences" and about a thousand customer success managers in the world; a decade later there were about 300,000. The first Pulse was 300 people in a room. By 2019 it was 5,500. Nick Mehta's line: "we didn't 'build' anything. We identified some seeds and tried to nurture them."

Taking: name the person, not the product. The host shows up for years. Source · Source
Analytics engineer · dbt · 2016Free artifact, silent vendor

A Slack before the company existed

The community started three months after the first commit and a month before the company. It doubled every year. The term "analytics engineer" was coined with friends and written down in 2019. The company ran a vendor-neutral newsletter, didn't promote its product in its own spaces, and its rule was "recognize, rather than incentivize." It reports that 80% of its inbound now comes from the community.

Taking: a free, portable artifact at the centre. The vendor stays out of the room. Source · Source
Trailblazers · Salesforce · 2006Careers

An identity people put on their résumé

It began with one person teaching admin classes and a customer volunteering to run the first local group. Today it's millions of members and 650+ local meetings across 70 countries. The number that matters: three in five members say the community helped them land a job or a promotion.

Taking: the identity has to be useful to a career, without a certificate. Source
AI engineer · Latent Space · 2023Essay, then a small room

One essay, one sold-out room of 500

A newsletter in 2022, an essay in June 2023 that named the role, and a first summit that October: 500 engineers, ten applicants per seat, a thousand pre-registrations in a day. The big event came the year after. Talks free online.

eCommerceFuel · 2012No vendors

The operator community that already works in ecommerce

You must own part of a store doing $1M+. "No vendors, no beginners." A thousand members in 39 countries, 50,000 searchable discussions, a live event of 300 operators and a weekly podcast since 2013. MACH Alliance · 2020Education, not apparatus

What a vendor alliance costs

Four companies founded it in June 2020 to teach the market a new architecture, and it worked: 111 members and 50 ambassadors by 2025. It also took a non-profit, an independent board, a managing director, certification and a flagship conference to get there, and five years. The pattern

Eight things every one of them did.

Named a person

Customer success manager, analytics engineer, AI engineer, Trailblazer. Not a product category. A job someone could be.

Wrote the text

A book, an essay, a post called "What is…". One canonical piece people could send each other.

Had distribution first

A newsletter, a Slack, dinners, a blog. The audience existed before the name did.

Kept the core free

Open source, free training, a free forum. The artifact at the centre was never gated.

Kept the vendor quiet

Peers in the room. The company that paid for it stayed out of the conversation, or out of the building.

Started small

300, 500, a few hundred. Sold out beats big. The big room came three or four years later.

Recognized, didn't pay

Names on work, a seat, a title. Not stipends.

Took years

Three years to a thousand people is the fast case. We review at ninety days and judge at a year.

Partners

Agencies and platforms. On the method, not on the money.

Operators don't run stores alone. The agencies that ship for them and the platforms they run on are part of how a store becomes AI-native, so they're part of this. One rule covers both: you take part by practising the method, not by paying for a logo.

There are no sponsorship packages, no reseller tiers and no referral fees here. If a partner has a commercial deal with any vendor, that's between them and the vendor and it stays off this site.
For agencies

The AI-native agency.

The agencies that will do well in the next five years are the ones whose clients run their stores with agents and still need them: for judgment, for the hard builds, for the things an agent shouldn't touch. The ones that will struggle are billing hours for work an agent now does in the morning run. We want the first kind in the room, and we'll say so publicly.

An agency qualifies by practice. If you work this way on at least one client store, you can hold a contributor seat, co-author the agency playbook, sit on the March session, and be listed here once there are five of you.

Five principles of an AI-native agency

  • An agent opens the first PR. Findings become pull requests before they become tickets. People review; they don't transcribe.
  • Every change ships with a before and after. A release marker and a number. The client never has to ask "did it work?"
  • The client owns the vault and the repo. The context file and the code live with the store, not with the agency. The client can leave with both.
  • Bill for outcomes, not for looking. Retainers, outcomes, fixed scopes. Not hours spent in a dashboard an agent reads for free.
  • Teach the client to Ask. The goal is a client who can ask the store a question. An agency that keeps that for itself isn't AI-native; it's gatekeeping.

What it isn't

Not a certification, not a directory you pay to be in, not a referral scheme. Signing the principles is free and self-attested. The listing comes from doing the work on a real store and putting a piece in the library.

For platforms

Shopify, Commerce, Klaviyo and the rest.

Platforms want their merchants to be AI-native as much as we do. They're also where operators actually live. So platforms get the two things that help their merchants, and none of the things that turn a community into a trade show.

The first platform session is being set up for the new year.

What a platform gets

  • A co-hosted session, with operators on their platform on the panel and their own team in the room.
  • Platform-specific skills and playbooks in the Stockroom: theme-preview A/B tests, flow agents, the pre-launch checklist for their checkout.
  • Their cut of the annual State of the AI-native Store report, by platform, to share with merchants.
  • The weekly drop, for their partner managers to forward.

What a platform doesn't get

Sponsorship, a logo on the site, a seat, or a say in what gets published. Tools that compete with each other can both be in the Stockroom. Their customers can hold seats. The companies can't.
